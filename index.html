An alternative vision for Canadian monetary policy

Keep the loonie as tax currency and shock absorber. Recognize US-regulated dollar stablecoins for US-dollar debts. Treat Bitcoin used as money, payroll, invoices, and settlement as currency, not a taxable disposition. No pegs. No federal Bitcoin reserve.

The frame that loses the room

A mainstream economist or portfolio manager does not need to bet on the end of fiat to take Bitcoin seriously. The gold-bug narrative demands exactly that: hyperbitcoinization is coming soon and sudden, the central bank is the enemy, and any year the Bank of Canada hits its inflation target is merely a delay. That frame is off-putting because it asks people with real mandates and real portfolios to adopt a collapse as the price of admission. It misses how Bitcoin actually improves the world while the loonie is still the wage and tax currency.

Friedman: the exit as a rule

The better frame is Friedman’s. A rule beats discretion. Bitcoin’s fixed issuance path is already written in a way no Ottawa committee can amend. It is not Canada’s k-rule, but it functions like an external benchmark. When Bitcoin use is taxed as a capital gain, the exit is legal but clumsy, so the Bank barely feels the pressure. Liberalize the transaction. Treat spending, payroll, and settlement in Bitcoin as currency use, not a disposition. A loose loonie policy then shows up immediately as people walking out the door. The issuer has a real incentive to behave as if a rule were binding. That is a k-rule result produced by competition, not by a new memo at the Bank.

The US dollar stablecoin

The same mechanism works against the US dollar. The regulated dollar stablecoin already exists in the United States. Canada does not need to invent one, and Canadian banks do not need to issue it or warehouse the reserves. Use the product Washington has already approved. A recognition rule is the update: a par-redeemable, US-regulated dollar stablecoin can settle a debt already written in US dollars. About seven in ten goods exports go south. Two-thirds of liquid reserves are already in dollars. Letting that stablecoin clear here is catching the statute up to the invoice. Canada stays a price taker on the instrument and keeps the loonie as the shock absorber. It is not full dollarization. It is just a practical policy for Canadians.

The shock absorber

The loonie tends to fall when the prices of the things Canada sells fall, and rise when those prices rise. Oil is the clean example. The same move reaches metals, lumber, potash, and a wide set of exports.

A firm paid in US dollars and paying wages in loonies gets part of a cheaper sale back at the border. A cheaper loonie also makes other exports easier to sell, so a region that missed the price hit can pick up some of the slack. Without that move, the shock lands on layoffs and wage cuts. Imports cost more. That is the price of the absorber, and it is still smaller than taking the whole hit in jobs.

Full dollarization removes the move. The wage is in US dollars too, so a cheaper export price is just a cheaper export price. The three-way book does not make that trade. The loonie still floats. A US-regulated dollar stablecoin can settle the invoice. Bitcoin is there for anyone who wants a stock neither government prints. The paycheck can stay in loonies. The invoice can clear in dollars. The savings can leave both.

Cobb-Douglas: a level, not a takeoff

This is a level shift, not a faster growth rate. Output is Y = AK^α L^(1-α), with α about one third. It comes from technology, capital, and labour. Canada borrows at a world rate, so the hurdle on a new project is set abroad.

Two costs sit on top of that hurdle. One is the capital-gains tax on using Bitcoin. The other is a US-dollar invoice that can only be settled in a foreign account. Canada is an exporting country, so those switches are routine, not exotic. The extra cost is a toll. The projects that die under a toll, even though the real return was already high enough, are a deadweight loss. Fewer projects get built than the economics justify.

Take those costs off and more projects get built. The capital stock gets a little deeper. The return on the next plant eases back toward the world rate. That is normal. More capital against the same required return means the next unit earns a bit less.

Bitcoin held as savings is not a machine. It does not count as capital. The help is simpler than that. Payroll and settlement can book in Canada, because a payment is no longer a tax event. And the profit those projects earn is harder to water down, because a household can leave either printer. Growth each year is still population plus productivity. The statute does not build the plant. It gets a toll out of the way.

Who holds it

This is Bitcoin as a micro tool for independent economic actors: households, firms, pensions, provinces, municipalities, reserves, and companies. They can hold it as their own private check on the loonie or the dollar. Ottawa and the Bank of Canada do not need a Bitcoin reserve. Nothing is backed by Bitcoin. Self-custody stays protected. Banks and fintechs can build on the rail without a public mandate to hold it.

Geography

Canada’s best advantage is still geography. We sit next to the world’s largest customer and the deepest capital market. Prosperity comes from competing hard on that relationship, not from retaliation or from pretending the loonie is the only money that counts. Bitcoin does not replace that relationship. It strengthens the hand of anyone who wants a fixed-supply savings asset alongside it.

The status quo has a constituency

A lot of mainstream opinion still treats the loonie, as it is now, as the safe answer. Canadian nationalism can make that feel like the patriotic answer too. Both slow the change down.

Some people in Canada do lose if the toll comes off. A bank that lives off the foreign account. A desk that lives off the tax filing. A gatekeeper whose product is the friction. That loss is real. It is not the same thing as a loss for the country. Clearance, payroll, and custody can book here instead of somewhere else. Households get a door. The float stays. Canada is better off on net.

You can keep the loonie without charging people to use the other two.

The proposal

The proposal is therefore modest on purpose. Keep the loonie as tax currency and shock absorber. Recognize US-regulated dollar stablecoins for US-dollar debts. Do not issue or custody a made-in-Canada dollar stablecoin. Treat Bitcoin used as money, payroll, invoices, and settlement, as currency, not a taxable disposition. No pegs. No federal Bitcoin reserve. No forced acceptance.

Closing thoughts

Long the door. Flat the prophecy. The exit makes dramatic endings less likely, not more. It simply raises the cost of bad policy while the real work, building plants, skills, and competitive industries, continues.

Bitcoin isn’t too volatile. You’re using the wrong yardstick.

Volatility is not a property of Bitcoin. It is a property of the pair. As long as prices, wages, and contracts are written in dollars, you are watching a fixed-supply rule get marked against a credit-elastic stock.

A mainstream economic framework

Bitcoin isn’t too volatile. You’re using the wrong yardstick.

Mainstream economists keep repeating the same sentence: Bitcoin is too volatile to be money. They are measuring the wrong thing. Volatility is not a property of Bitcoin. It is a property of the pair. As long as prices, wages, and contracts are written in dollars, you are watching a fixed-supply rule get marked against a credit-elastic stock. That cross-rate will keep moving. That is not a bug in Bitcoin. That is the test.

Two policies, one price

Bitcoin has a monetary rule: a capped supply, issuance that declines on a schedule, no committee that can print. The dollar has an authority: rates, QE and QT, deficits, bank credit, M2. U.S. M2 is now about $23 trillion and growing again. The large-economy money stock is in the $100 trillion range. Bitcoin’s entire market is still a sliver of that.

The BTC/USD price is the market equating those two stocks every second. There is no peg. There is no official bid. Discovery is public. A managed currency hides the adjustment in reserves, inflation, or capital controls. Bitcoin puts the whole adjustment in the price. Of course the pair looks lively. Gold looked “volatile” after 1971 for the same reason: the metal did not suddenly become erratic. The unit it was quoted in started to float.

Money does three jobs — not all at once

Textbooks give money three functions: store of value, medium of exchange, unit of account. Bitcoin is doing the first job now. That is the product. People and firms volunteer to hold a bearer claim that cannot be diluted by the same process that expands M2.

It is not yet the yardstick. Almost no one writes rent, payroll, or a mortgage in sats. Businesses that accept bitcoin still price in fiat. Judging a store of value by unit-of-account criteria is a category error. Gold was a reserve and a settlement asset long before most prices were written in ounces. The dollar is a unit of account by law and habit. Its record as a store of value is why the second option exists.

You do not need all three functions on day one. Sequencing is how money actually spreads: save first, settle where it is cheaper, denominate last.

Relativity vs volatility

Quote bitcoin in dollars and bitcoin looks unstable. Quote a house, a wage, or a Treasury in bitcoin and the dollar looks unstable. Nothing in the good changed. The measuring stick did. Emerging-market currencies look chaotic for the same reason: we quote them in dollars.

Two limits keep this honest. First, the switch — if it comes — is gradual. A parallel system can gain share for a long time while fiat still clears most of GDP. Second, bitcoin’s own dollar volatility has already compressed as the market matured. Deeper books and longer-duration holders can calm the series without erasing the policy mismatch. The pair stays high-beta because one stock is a rule and the other is a choice made in treasuries and banks.

A parallel system, not a failed dollar

Bitcoin is not a broken version of the existing financial system. It is a parallel one. It has its own currency, its own monetary policy, and its own settlement: final transfer of a bearer claim, without a commercial bank or a central bank as the clearing house. Anyone can hold the reserve asset directly. Public companies that put bitcoin on the balance sheet are just the listed version of that choice.

The dollar system still clears wages, taxes, most debt, and most GDP. Coexistence is the base case, not a halfway house. The BTC/USD rate is simply the floating exchange rate between the two regimes.

That stack already hints at a division of labor. Bitcoin is the thing you save and settle in. An elastic token — bank deposits, T-bill funds, a regulated stablecoin — is the thing you spend. People who refuse to hoard the elastic token will turn it over quickly; high velocity in the spending stock is what you get when the savings asset sits next door. You already see the edge of this in treasury desks, remittances, and cross-border settlement: a neutral claim that does not require either side to hold the other country’s credit money.

Bitcoin doesn’t solve macroeconomic problems

Bitcoin is often sold as a macroeconomic tool: the thing that will fix inflation, close the deficit, or replace the central bank. That is the wrong scale. Participation is voluntary. Nobody is drafted, and nobody adopts it by decree. A household or a firm decides whether to hold part of its savings under a rule instead of under an authority. That is a portfolio decision and a property-rights decision. It is microeconomics.

It does not set the policy rate, tax, run the labor market, or manage aggregate demand. It protects the chooser.

The exit option still has a macroeconomic shadow, and it should be stated carefully. If balances can leave the local unit for a bearer reserve, bad policy gets more expensive. That is capital mobility, not a forecast that fiat states vanish. The cost of debasement becomes more visible when a parallel reserve exists.

Time and market share change the numeraire. They do not need to abolish the dollar first. Until they do, economists will keep measuring bitcoin in dollars and calling the measuring stick “Bitcoin’s volatility.” Inside the parallel system the policy is already stable. What gets called Bitcoin’s volatility is the dollar moving against that rule.

Bitcoin’s true value isn’t apocalypse insurance

Bitcoin’s true significance is not limited to crisis scenarios. It remains highly valuable even in a world where governments act responsibly. Absolute scarcity enforced by code, not policy.

Bitcoin’s True Value Isn’t Apocalypse Insurance — It’s Something Much More Important

For years, Bitcoin’s story has been dominated by an apocalyptic narrative. The idea that governments will destroy their currencies through reckless money printing, leading to fiat’s collapse, has been heavily promoted not only by traditional gold bugs, but also by many in the Austrian school of economics. In this view, Bitcoin is positioned as the money that will take over once the current system inevitably fails.

This doomer framing, however, misses Bitcoin’s deeper and more lasting importance.

Bitcoin’s true significance is not limited to crisis scenarios. It remains highly valuable even in a world where governments act responsibly. In fact, Bitcoin can comfortably fit within a traditional monetarist and neoclassical economic framework.

Consider a stable economic environment where governments run fiscal surpluses, keep inflation low and predictable, and follow a steady monetary rule similar to Milton Friedman’s k-percent rule.

In such a world, fiat would continue to serve effectively as the main medium of exchange for daily transactions. Bitcoin, by contrast, would function as a parallel monetary system — a superior store of value and long-term savings technology.

Even under a disciplined fiat regime, Bitcoin offers a unique advantage that no government currency can match: absolute scarcity. While a responsible central bank may follow a monetary rule today, that rule is ultimately enforced by human institutions and can be changed tomorrow. Bitcoin’s fixed supply of 21 million coins is enforced by mathematics and decentralized consensus — it cannot be altered.

This distinction between relative scarcity (managed by policy) and absolute scarcity (enforced by code) is powerful. Beyond scarcity, Bitcoin provides practical microeconomic benefits: it is borderless, censorship-resistant, seizure-resistant, perfectly divisible, and instantly verifiable. These features reduce transaction costs and strengthen property rights, concepts well-understood in neoclassical economics.

Historical examples support this view. Scotland’s free banking period and the 19th-century U.S. free banking era both saw private currencies successfully operating in parallel with official money. The Eurodollar market later demonstrated how a parallel financial system could thrive alongside traditional banking without replacing it.

The healthiest outcome is not monetary collapse, but peaceful coexistence. When governments accept Bitcoin’s presence and respond by maintaining monetary discipline, citizens gain access to both a stable domestic currency for daily use and a neutral, hard asset for preserving long-term wealth.

Bitcoin, understood this way, is not a radical rejection of mainstream economics. It aligns naturally with monetarist principles of rules-based money and neoclassical ideas around property rights, transaction costs, and individual choice.

The strongest case for Bitcoin is not that fiat will fail. It is that Bitcoin represents better money — even when fiat is working at its best.
